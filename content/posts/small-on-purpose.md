We'd rather have twenty people who know each other's work than two hundred who don't. That's one of the five values we wrote down before our first gathering, and it shapes almost every decision we make.

## Trust is the product

The best conversations we've had about building software happened in small rooms, with people who had already seen each other's work. When you know the person across the table shipped something hard last year, you listen differently. You ask better questions. You admit what went wrong.

That kind of trust doesn't scale with headcount. So we don't try to.

## What small rules out

Keeping the room small means saying no to a few things that would be easy to say yes to:

- **Growth for its own sake.** A bigger mailing list is not the goal. A better room is.
- **Becoming a chapter of something bigger.** We're here for the Island, and we want to stay that way.
- **Keynotes and vendor pitches.** If you come to share, show the real work, messy parts included.

## Show the work, and leave it behind

Every gathering should leave the group with something it keeps: a workflow, a template, a lesson. Small rooms make that possible, because there's time for everyone to ask the follow-up question.

> We'd rather have twenty people who know each other's work than two hundred who don't.
>
> — ProductHub Victoria values
